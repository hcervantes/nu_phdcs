## Slide 3 — What Is Artificial Intelligence?

Let's start with a working definition. Artificial intelligence is broadly defined as intelligence demonstrated by machines — systems that emulate the cognitive functions humans perform. In practice, that means machines that can learn to solve problems, predict future outcomes, and make decisions based on what they have learned. The crucial ingredient is knowledge acquisition: once exposed to an environment, a machine collects data as input, processes it, and draws conclusions much as a person would. Think of a navigation app that learns traffic patterns over time, or a medical diagnostic system that grows more accurate with every case it reviews.

The insight here is that intelligence in AI is not magic — it is a repeatable cycle of input, processing, and adaptation. Researchers often distinguish three flavors of AI, as Huang and Rust describe: mechanical AI that executes well-defined transactional tasks; thinking AI that augments human analysis and reasoning; and feeling AI that engages emotional and social tasks. That last category — feeling AI — is where this presentation gets most interesting, because it sets up two themes we'll keep returning to: how humanlike should machines be, and what happens when they become humanlike enough that we respond to them socially, even emotionally?

Those questions are precisely where the modern research literature concentrates, and they're the focus of our next section. Before we move on, hold onto this core definition — machine-emulated cognition — because neural networks and machine learning are the technical tools that make it possible.

## Slide 4 — Anthropomorphism: Making Machines More Human

Anthropomorphism is the practice of ascribing human traits to nonhuman things — and it is everywhere in AI design. Social robots and humanlike interfaces are built to interact with us in humanlike ways because we respond socially to social cues. The rationale is practical: humanlike forms mirror human cognitive tendencies in the real world, making interactions smoother, more intuitive, and more trusted.

Salles, Evers, and Farisco, writing in *AJOB Neuroscience* in 2020, note that as AI research has surged, so have ethical concerns about this practice — concerns specifically about deception, nefarious intent, and behavior outside human control. If a robot appears to experience authentic feelings, are we being manipulated? But anthropomorphism also has genuinely beneficial applications. Consider social assistive robots — SARs — designed to emulate caregivers for individuals with Alzheimer's disease experiencing cognitive decline. These robots cannot replace a human caregiver, yet research shows that reliance on such assistive technology produces promising benefits: medication reminders, companionship cues, and consistent, patient interaction around the clock.

The takeaway here is a balanced one: anthropomorphism is a deliberate design choice with real benefits for engagement, communication, and care — and real risks when the humanlike appearance of a machine outruns its actual capabilities, or when emotional simulation is used to influence users without their awareness. Researchers study not just whether to humanize machines, but how much, in which contexts, and with what safeguards. On the next slide, we'll look at striking evidence that humanlike AI doesn't just change behavior — it may change how users see themselves.

## Slide 5 — Beyond Adoption: AI and User Identity

Now we examine contrasting viewpoints in the literature. Alabed, Javornik, and Gregory-Smith, publishing in *Technological Forecasting and Social Change* in 2022, argue that humanlike AI can shape users' identities — not just their willingness to adopt a technology. Their framework describes three families of anthropomorphic cues: physical cues such as appearance, voice, and name; personality cues; and emotional cues. These cues can produce self-congruence, where users perceive the AI as similar to themselves. And self-congruence may progress to what the authors call self-AI integration — users coming to see an AI agent as meaningful enough to become part of their own self-concept.

Who is most susceptible? The framework points to extraverts, innovators, and people with a strong need to belong; familiar or positively framed agents amplify congruence; and people with interdependent self-views — or those experiencing social exclusion — may be especially likely to integrate AI into their identity. The outcomes cut both ways. Benefits include stronger emotional connection, perceived support, improved social participation, and societal gains when routine cognitive work is delegated to AI. Risks include intimate self-disclosure that threatens privacy, weakened human empathy and reciprocity through over-reliance on AI companions, and what the authors term digital dementia — reduced cognitive capability from excessive delegation of thinking to machines.

Notice how this contrasts with the care-robot evidence on the prior slide: there, people clearly distinguished humans from machines in intimate settings, and anthropomorphism aided communication but not deeper acceptance. The same humanlike design can build trust or exploit vulnerability depending on context. Designers, the authors conclude, must personalize appropriately, communicate capabilities transparently, and avoid manufactured intimacy. From why machines feel human, we now turn to how they learn.

## Slide 6 — Neural Networks: The Brain-Inspired Architecture

A neural network is a computing system made of interconnected nodes, inspired by the structure of the human brain — neurons communicating across synapses. Artificial neural networks rely on algorithms to detect hidden patterns and associations in data, and based on those findings, they group and classify data over time as learning occurs. The biological analogy is worth pausing on. The human brain exhibits neuroplasticity: when a region is damaged, biological processes detect the disruption and reroute signals to working areas. Neural networks borrow this insight — their strength is not any single computation but continuous adaptation. As new data flows through, the connection weights adjust so performance improves with experience, without anyone explicitly reprogramming the system.

What is the purpose of a neural network in intelligent decision-making? The overarching goal is to solve complex real-world problems with accuracy that meets or exceeds a human addressing the same problem. That benchmark — matching or exceeding human performance — is what data scientists, researchers, and practitioners care about. The payoff is a system that keeps improving on its own; the network's learning loop refines it continuously. This is why neural networks appear across so many domains today: medical diagnosis, quality control, demand forecasting, targeted marketing, and fraud detection — applications where patterns are subtle, data is plentiful, and the cost of a wrong decision is high.

On the next slide, I'll walk through how information actually flows through one of these networks, and how that flow mirrors what neuroscientists observe in the functioning human brain.

## Slide 7 — How a Neural Network Thinks

This diagram walks through the structure and signal flow of an artificial neural network. Reading left to right, there are three kinds of layers. The input layer receives raw data — imagine pixel values from an image, or a customer's transaction history. Each input node is like a receptor, holding one numerical value. These signals travel along weighted connections to the hidden layers — the network's engine room. Each hidden node multiplies the incoming values by their weights, sums them, and passes the result through an activation function that determines how strongly to fire — much as a biological neuron fires only when incoming stimulation passes an electrochemical threshold. Finally, the output layer produces the result: a classification, a prediction, a decision. For a facial expression task, the output might be a probability distribution: 82 percent happy, 11 percent neutral, 7 percent sad.

How does learning occur? In the forward pass, data flows left to right and the network makes its best guess. That guess is compared to the correct answer, producing an error. Then comes the feedback loop: the error propagates backward through the network, and each connection weight is nudged in the direction that would reduce that error. Repeat this across thousands or millions of examples, and the weights converge to a configuration that captures the underlying pattern.

The brain parallel is direct: neuroplasticity means failed signal pathways are rerouted after trauma — a form of biological re-weighting. Backpropagation is the computational analog: failed predictions trigger weight adjustments that redirect the information flow. Neither system is programmed with the answer; both adapt from experience. For the NetworkX diagram, a clean model uses a layered feedforward structure — three input nodes, two hidden layers of four nodes each, and two output nodes — with edge width scaled by weight and a dashed backpropagation arrow curving along the bottom from output back to input.

## Slide 8 — Neural Networks in the Real World

Let's ground the architecture in real applications. In healthcare operations, researchers are applying intelligent algorithms to complex scheduling and coordination decisions. Torabzadeh and colleagues, in *Computers in Biology and Medicine* in 2022, present an intelligent algorithm to evaluate and improve the performance of a home healthcare center incorporating trust indicators — a domain where scheduling, caregiver matching, and reliability all interact simultaneously, and where a learning system outperforms static rules. In clinical diagnosis broadly, neural networks flag anomalies in medical imaging and lab results, supporting earlier and more accurate intervention for patients.

In finance, fraud detection is a flagship case: a network learns each cardholder's normal transaction fingerprint and scores deviations in milliseconds — a pattern task too vast and subtle for hand-written rules to manage. Quality control in manufacturing uses networks to spot defects visually on production lines faster and more consistently than human inspectors. Demand forecasting in retail and targeted marketing both depend on networks finding hidden associations in behavioral data — the engine behind every streaming and shopping recommendation delivered to a user.

The common thread across every application: the network's value comes from continuous learning, with accuracy improving as data accumulates, and from relieving people of pattern-heavy, high-volume work so human attention can go to judgment, care, and edge cases that require it. And in every application, the network is ultimately making decisions that affect real people — which makes understanding how it learns not a technical curiosity but a matter of practical and ethical importance. That learning process is machine learning, and it comes in four fundamental forms.

## Slide 9 — Machine Learning: Supervised vs. Unsupervised

Machine learning is the toolbox that powers AI, and its techniques divide primarily by how much guidance the algorithm receives. Supervised learning is learning with a teacher: the algorithm trains on labeled examples — inputs paired with correct answers. Spam filtering is the classic illustration: thousands of emails pre-marked spam or not-spam teach a model the distinguishing features, after which it labels new mail itself. Fraud detection, medical diagnosis from labeled scans, and price prediction are all supervised tasks. The strength is accuracy when targets are clear; the cost is the labor of producing labeled data at scale.

Unsupervised learning, by contrast, receives no answers at all — only raw, unlabeled data. Its job is to discover hidden structure on its own. The signature technique is clustering: grouping data points that resemble one another without being told what the groups should look like. Customer segmentation is the everyday example — a retailer feeds in browsing and purchase behavior, and clusters emerge organically: bargain hunters, loyal regulars, one-time gift buyers — categories the business never explicitly defined in advance.

Sarker's 2021 review in *SN Computer Science* catalogues these algorithm families and their real-world applications, providing a useful peer-reviewed anchor for this comparison. When would you choose each approach? Supervised learning fits situations where the output categories are known and labeled data is available. Unsupervised learning fits situations where you suspect structure in the data but don't know its shape — for exploratory analysis, anomaly detection, or preprocessing for downstream models. The trade-off mirrors a familiar dynamic: supervised learning is efficient but bounded by its teacher's knowledge, while unsupervised learning is free to surface patterns no human thought to look for — sometimes revealing genuinely novel insight, sometimes surfacing noise that requires careful interpretation.

## Slide 10 — Machine Learning: Reinforcement & Semi-Supervised

Reinforcement learning is learning by trial, error, and reward — with no labels and no teacher. An agent acts in an environment, receives feedback in the form of rewards or penalties, and gradually discovers the strategy that maximizes long-term reward. The vivid illustration is game-playing AI: the OpenAI Five system played the equivalent of hundreds of years of games against itself every single day, improving through nothing but self-play and the win-or-lose signal. Robotics uses reinforcement learning to teach movement and manipulation; recommendation systems use it to maximize long-term user engagement; and autonomous-vehicle simulation uses it to learn driving policies under safety constraints before any real-road contact occurs.

The challenge — particularly relevant for Emotion AI — is that emotional reward signals are sparse, delayed, and genuinely difficult to define. A system trying to maximize human wellbeing in a social interaction doesn't receive a clean score the way a chess engine does, which makes this optimization problem significantly harder.

Semi-supervised learning addresses a very practical pain point: labeled data is expensive and time-consuming to produce, but unlabeled data is everywhere. The approach trains on a small labeled set and a large unlabeled set together — the labeled portion anchors the model's understanding while the unlabeled portion reveals the data's natural shape and density. Typical applications include image recognition, where a few thousand labeled photos guide classification across millions of unlabeled ones, and speech or document classification where human annotation cannot keep pace with the volume of incoming data. A direct connection back to neural networks: Lin, Bouneffouf, and Cecchi, in *PLOS ONE* in 2022, used recurrent neural networks to predict human decision-making in psychological tasks — an application of deep learning to understanding human cognition itself. Together, the four techniques form a complete spectrum of guidance. That foundation now positions the most human question in this entire presentation — can machines sense feelings?

## Slide 11 — Emotion AI: Detecting Human Feelings

Emotion AI is a form of artificial intelligence designed to detect and interpret human emotional signals. Hortensius, Hekele, and Cross, writing in *IEEE Transactions on Cognitive and Developmental Systems* in 2018, frame the central scientific question: how accurately can humans perceive emotions exhibited by artificial agents — and how do we respond when they do? That question carries both technical and social dimensions, and both matter for responsible deployment.

Emotion AI systems operate across several signal channels. Sentiment analysis reads text — is this customer review expressing delight or frustration? Audio analysis infers emotion from tone, pitch, pace, and energy of voice. Video analysis reads facial expressions, mapping muscle movements to emotional categories based on foundational cross-cultural research into universal expressions. And behavior-based systems study gait and physiological signals — galvanic skin response, heart rate, movement patterns — as additional emotional indicators.

Affective computing uses these detections to modulate an agent's response in real time. Picture a person lost on a subway platform approaching a robot for directions. By detecting stress in the human's voice and posture, the robot can adjust its behavior: slowing its pace, softening its tone, giving clear step-by-step instructions — de-escalating the interaction rather than simply delivering information mechanically. Emotions play an integral role in intent, perception, and cognition; they shape whether an agent's help actually lands, or whether it makes a frustrating situation worse.

But the research also surfaces real design risks. A robot exhibiting something resembling anger could be perceived as threatening. A customer-service chatbot with an overly positive tone might come across as condescending to a disgruntled caller. The accuracy of human perception of machine emotion, and the design of contextually appropriate emotional responses, are exactly the areas the literature identifies as most urgently needing further study.

## Slide 12 — Emotion AI in Practice: Tutoring & Care

Where is emotion-aware AI already making a measurable difference? Education is a leading domain. Channa and colleagues, in *Elementary Education Online* in 2021, discuss supporting self-regulated learning through affect detection and responsive AI-driven systems. Emotions dramatically influence cognitive functions — attention, decision-making, reasoning, and memory recall — and an intelligent tutoring system that can sense an individual's affective state — boredom, confusion, frustration, or anxiety — and respond to it, maintains the learning flow rather than pushing ahead regardless of engagement. Rather than one-size-fits-all instruction, an affect-sensitive system adapts its pacing, content, and tone to the emotional state of the individual, fostering deeper thinking and problem-solving. The paper is candid about limitations: detecting affect reliably remains technically difficult, and improved outcomes are not yet guaranteed — an honest gap the field is working to close.

Care for older adults is the second domain. Engel's 2022 edited volume, *Robots in Care and Everyday Life*, surveys social acceptance and ethics of care robots. Its Bremen survey found that robots with humanlike attributes — Care-O-bot 4 and Pepper — were associated with greater willingness to converse with robots, though not greater acceptance of robots in care roles overall. People consistently resisted imagining robots in intimate roles: everyday personal conversation, discussions about loneliness or family matters, or replacing a pet.

The synthesis of both sources is clear: emotional capability in AI is most effective as support — a system that adapts to frustration, or a companion that eases isolation — while the need for genuine empathy, human recognition, and human contact remains distinctly outside the current reach of machines. That boundary sets up the ethical close to this presentation.

## Slide 13 — Ethics & the Research Frontier

As we bring the threads of Assignment 7 together, three ethical imperatives and four research agendas emerge from the peer-reviewed literature. First, transparency and human dignity. Anthropomorphism can lead people — particularly those in care settings — to treat a machine as a humanlike confidant or relationship substitute. The ethics literature, including Engel's 2022 volume, consistently emphasizes preserving autonomy, dignity, privacy, and the right to human contact. AI systems should support care and everyday activity without obscuring their machine status or displacing human relationships and human responsibility.

Second, identity and the risk of manipulation. Alabed and colleagues remind us that humanlike AI can integrate into users' self-concepts, with real benefits — connection, support, social participation — and equally real risks: privacy loss through intimate disclosure, weakened human empathy and reciprocity, and digital dementia from over-delegated thinking. Designers carry a clear responsibility here: personalization should deepen genuine engagement, not manufacture intimacy or exploit psychological vulnerability.

Third, measurement and validity. The perception of emotion in artificial agents — how accurate it is, and how diverse populations respond — remains under-explored, as Hortensius and colleagues note. Emotion AI must be rigorously evaluated before deployment at scale, especially with vulnerable populations such as individuals with cognitive decline.

For the research frontier, each section of Assignment 7 surfaces a clear agenda. In AI broadly: which levels and types of anthropomorphism help versus harm, across diverse cultural and individual contexts. In neural networks: interpretability — understanding why a network makes the decision it makes — and architectures that generalize well from smaller datasets. In machine learning: the semi-supervised frontier and safe learning from human feedback. In emotion AI: reliable affect detection across demographics, ethical display rules for machine emotion, and longitudinal evidence on user outcomes. The common thread: keep human autonomy, dignity, and connection at the center of every design decision.

This mirrors the brain's own neuroplasticity: connections that fail are rerouted; connections that succeed are strengthened.

We compared the four machine learning techniques on a spectrum of guidance — supervised learning with labeled answers, unsupervised learning discovering hidden structure, reinforcement learning driven by reward, and semi-supervised learning blending a small labeled set with a large unlabeled one — with everyday examples from spam filters and customer segmentation to game-playing AI and human-decision prediction using recurrent networks.

And we explored the most human dimension of this field: emotion. How AI detects it through text, voice, face, and behavior. Why we emulate it in educational tutors and care companions. And where its limits lie — genuine empathy, human recognition, and human connection remain distinctly outside what machines can authentically provide.

The single idea I hope carries forward from this presentation: these four approaches are converging into machines that learn, decide, and now respond to feelings. The decisive question for researchers and for society is not just what these machines can do — but what they *should* do, and how their design keeps human autonomy, dignity, and connection at the center.
