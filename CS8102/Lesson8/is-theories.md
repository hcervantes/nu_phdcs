## General Systems Theory (GST) <a name="general-systems-theory-gst"></a>

*Source: [IS Wiki — General systems theory](https://is.theorizeit.org/wiki/General_systems_theory)*

### Origin & Key Authors

- Proposed in **1936 by Austrian biologist Ludwig von Bertalanffy**; further developed by **Ross Ashby**
- Von Bertalanffy was **reacting against reductionism** and seeking to revive the unity of science
- Formalized mid-century with economists and mathematicians: **Kenneth Boulding, Ralph Gerard, Anatol Rapoport** (Society for General Systems Research, 1954; now ISSS)
- Landmark works: von Bertalanffy's *General System Theory* (1968); Boulding's "General Systems Theory — The Skeleton of Science" (1956)

### Core Idea

- A **system** is a set of interconnected elements that form a complex whole
- GST is a "**general science of wholeness**": systems must be studied **holistically**, as organized wholes whose properties emerge from the dynamic relations among components — not by analyzing parts in isolation
- Key principle: **everything is connected to everything else** — a change in one part affects the whole
- The same systems principles apply **isomorphically across domains** (biological, social, technological, economic), which is why GST unifies sciences

### Key Concepts

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Open system** | Exchanges matter, energy, and information with its environment (vs. closed system) |
| **System–environment boundary** | Separates the system from its environment |
| **Input / Process / Output** | How the system transforms what it receives |
| **State** | The system's condition at a point in time |
| **Hierarchy** | Systems are composed of subsystems and nested within suprasystems |
| **Goal-directedness** | Systems pursue goals or steady states |
| **Information & feedback** | Communication and regulatory feedback within/between systems |
| **Emergence** | Whole-system properties that arise from component interactions |
| **Equifinality** | Different initial conditions can reach the same final state |

### Relation to Other Theories

- **Cybernetics** is related but distinct: cybernetics is the theory of control mechanisms, information, and feedback; GST is broader — a general paradigm for studying organization and wholeness
- **Socio-technical theory** builds directly on GST's open-systems foundation
- An **information system is an example of GST in action** — the interaction of multiple interdependent elements

### Relevance to Information Systems & Assignment 8

- The IS wiki notes that an IS is itself an instantiation of GST: multiple interacting elements
- GST is the theoretical backbone for the paper's required **systems theory perspective**: it explains how computing systems interconnect with non-computing systems (biological, family, societal, economic)
- Directly matches Lesson 8's systems discussion: the GPU–CPU–motherboard interdependence example, and the intersection of technical and natural-world systems
- Pairs with the lesson's Figure 1 (Sayama's Complex Systems Organization Map, CC BY-SA)

## Socio-technical Theory (STS) <a name="socio-technical-theory-sts"></a>

*Source: [IS Wiki — Socio-technical theory](https://is.theorizeit.org/wiki/Socio-technical_theory)*

### Origin & Key Authors

- Developed in the **1950s at the Tavistock Institute** in London
- Founding work: **Trist & Bamforth (1951)**, from field studies of British coal mining
- Later contributors: **Fred Emery, Enid Mumford, Albert Cherns**, and colleagues
- Built on **general systems theory / open systems theory** (von Bertalanffy, 1950)

### Core Idea

A work system consists of two interdependent subsystems:

| Subsystem | Includes |
|---|---|
| **Technical** | Tools, machines, tasks, techniques, devices, control functions |
| **Social** | People, roles, relationships, skills, knowledge, norms |

System success comes from **joint optimization** — designing and optimizing both subsystems *together*. Optimizing the technical at the expense of the social (or vice versa) produces suboptimal or failed systems.

### Why It Emerged

- Mid-20th-century optimism that technology alone would drive efficiency was being confounded — technology introductions often *degraded* productivity and worker well-being
- STS rejected the **technological imperative** (people as extensions of machines)
- Reframed people as **"a resource to be developed,"** not an expendable spare part

### Key Concepts

- **Joint optimization** — social and technical subsystems must be designed together
- **Open systems foundation** — the system exchanges with its environment; success depends on responding to environmental demands
- **Equifinality** — multiple designs can achieve the same steady state
- **Dual shaping** — the social and technical subsystems shape each other reciprocally
- **Environmental complexity** — psychological, economic, technical, cultural, and political dynamics must be understood together

### Relevance to Information Systems

- Explains why system failures often stem from ignoring the **social subsystem** in technical design
- Directly supports the "systems theory perspective" required in Assignment 8: it links computing systems (technical subsystem) with non-computing systems (social, organizational, societal)
- Pairs well with **stakeholder theory** for analyzing who is affected by technical design decisions

- Pasmore, W. A., Francis, C., Haldeman, J., & Shani, A. B. (1982). Sociotechnical systems: A North American reflection on empirical studies of the seventies. *Human Relations, 35*(12), 1179–1204.

## Stakeholder Theory <a name="stakeholder-theory"></a>

*Source: [IS Wiki — Stakeholder theory](https://is.theorizeit.org/wiki/Stakeholder_theory)*

### Origin & Key Authors

- Term first used in a **1963 Stanford Research Institute memo**: stakeholders are "groups without whose support the organization would cease to exist"
- Fully developed and championed by **R. Edward Freeman** in *Strategic Management: A Stakeholder Approach* (1984)
- Major extensions: Frooman (1999) — stakeholder influence strategies; Hill & Jones (1992) — stakeholder-agency theory; Jawahar & McLaughlin (2001) — organizational life-cycle approach

### Core Idea

- A firm's purpose is to serve and coordinate the interests of **all** its stakeholders — not shareholders alone
- **Stakeholders** are any groups (or individuals) who can affect or are affected by the achievement of the organization's objectives: employees, customers, suppliers, financiers, communities, governments, trade unions, even competitors
- The theory addresses the "**Principle of Who or What Really Counts**": it both *describes* stakeholder groups and *recommends* methods by which management gives due regard to their interests
- It is simultaneously a **theory of management** *and* a **theory of business ethics** — it explicitly embeds morals and values into organizational decision-making

### Contrast with the Shareholder View

| Shareholder (traditional) view | Stakeholder view |
|---|---|
| Firm's fiduciary duty is to maximize shareholder value | Firm must balance interests of all affected parties |
| Other parties are a means to shareholder returns | All legitimate stakeholder interests have independent standing |
| Narrow accountability | Broad accountability, including societal effects |

### Key Concepts

- **Stakeholder identification & salience** — who counts, and how much (power, legitimacy, urgency)
- **Stakeholder influence strategies** — how stakeholders shape firm behavior (Frooman, 1999)
- **Primary vs. secondary stakeholders** — direct/voluntary relationships vs. indirect ones
- **Normative core** — stakeholders' interests have intrinsic value, not merely instrumental worth

### Application in IS Research

The IS wiki catalog highlights stakeholder theory's use across information systems topics:

- **ICT and organizational performance** — e.g., Dimovski & Skerlavaj (2004) modeled organizational ICT performance through a stakeholder lens
- **E-government projects** — managing stakeholder relationships in system implementation (e.g., Pardo et al., AMCIS 2003)
- **E-commerce research evolution** — Chua, Straub, Khoo, Kadiyala, & Kuechler (2005) traced e-commerce research development from a stakeholder perspective

### Relevance to Assignment 8

- The **best anchor theory for Prompt 2** (individuality and human rights): identifies who is affected by emerging technologies
- Directly connects to your EBSCO reading: Trittin-Ulbrich & Martin's "**unaware stakeholders**" of digital technology (people whose data trains AI or who face automated decisions without knowing it) challenges and extends Freeman's original definition
- Supports the human-centric approach to digital technologies and the accountability arguments against the "inscrutability" fallacy
- Pairs well with **socio-technical theory** (who the social subsystem includes) and the **ACM Code** (professional duty to stakeholders harmed by computing systems)

## Selective Organizational Information Privacy and Security Violations Model (SOIPSVM) <a name="soipsvm"></a>

*Source: [IS Wiki — SOIPSVM](https://is.theorizeit.org/wiki/Selective_organizational_information_privacy_and_security_violations_model_(SOIPSVM)) · [JAIS article](https://aisel.aisnet.org/jais/vol17/iss1/4/)*

### Origin & Key Authors

- Proposed in the *Journal of the Association for Information Systems* (2016) — *"Organizational Violations of Externally Governed Privacy and Security Rules: Explaining and Predicting Selective Violations under Conditions of Strain and Excess"*
- Builds on and extends the **Selective Organizational Rule Violations Model (SORVM)**

### Core Idea

- Explains how **organizational structures and processes**, together with **characteristics of regulatory rules**, alter risk perceptions when an organization's **performance falls short of its aspiration levels**, thereby affecting the likelihood of **rule violations** in privacy and security
- Central concept: **selectivity** — organizations do not violate rules randomly; violations are **dynamic and selective**, yet influenced by external forces (which rules are broken, when, and under what conditions)

### Key Concepts

- **Aspiration levels** — gaps between performance and goals drive selective deviance
- **Strain vs. slack** — extends SORVM beyond organizations under strain (e.g., economic hardship) to organizations with **slack resources** that still violate rules opportunistically
- **Regulatory rule characteristics** — rule clarity, enforcement, and sanctions shape perceived risk
- **External governance** — the role of external regulators and compliance frameworks

### Four Contributions to Privacy/Security Research

1. Introduces **selectivity** of rule violations to privacy and security research
2. **Extends SORVM's boundaries** — selectivity applies also to organizations with slack resources, not just those under strain
3. Improves **prediction** of which organizations will violate which rules and when
4. Offers **practical guidance** for regulators designing more violation-resistant rules

## Complexity Theory <a name="complexity-theory"></a>

*Source: [IS Wiki — Complexity theory](https://is.theorizeit.org/wiki/Complexity_theory)*

### Origin & Key Authors

- Founded by **Juris Hartmanis and Richard E. Stearns** — *"On the computational complexity of algorithms"* (*Transactions of the AMS*, 117, 1965, 285–306), which earned them the 1993 **ACM Turing Award**
- Further developed in IS contexts by **Schneberger & McLean** ("The Complexity Cross: Implications for Practice," *Communications of the ACM*, 2003)

### Core Idea (as defined on the IS wiki)

- Part of the **theory of computation** dealing with the **resources required during computation** to solve a given problem
- A problem is considered inherently difficult if solving it requires **significant resources**, regardless of the algorithm used

### Key Concepts

| Resource | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Time complexity** | How many computational steps a problem requires |
| **Space complexity** | How much memory a problem requires |
| **Parallelism** | How many processors are needed for parallel solutions |
| **Other measures** | Communication, circuit gates, number of processors |

- **Not to be confused** with complex adaptive systems / complexity science used in organization studies — the IS wiki page focuses on the **computational** sense (a related organizational-strategy sense exists in IS via Schneberger & McLean's "Complexity Cross," mapping problem/technology complexity to development strategy)

### IS Applications

- Network design with survivability constraints (Myung, Kim, & Tcha, 1999)
- Scalable, distributed multicast security protocols (Aslan, 2004)
- Determining the practical limits of what computers can and cannot do — relevant to algorithmic feasibility in IS design

## Institutional Theory <a name="institutional-theory"></a>

*Source: [IS Wiki — Institutional theory](https://is.theorizeit.org/wiki/Institutional_theory) · [Wikipedia: Institutional theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutional_theory)*

### Origin & Key Authors

- Roots in **old institutionalism** in sociology and political science
- **"New institutionalism"** relaunched by **Meyer & Rowan (1977)** and consolidated by **DiMaggio & Powell (1983, 1991)**
- **W. Richard Scott (1995, 2001)** — the standard synthesis of the field
- Later extensions: **institutional logics** (Thornton, Ocasio & Lounsbury, 2012), **institutional work** (Lawrence & Suddaby, 2006)

### Core Idea

- Explains how **structures, schemes, rules, norms, and routines** become established as authoritative guidelines for social behavior
- To survive, organizations must **conform to the rules and belief systems prevailing in their environment** — because **isomorphism earns legitimacy**
- Organizations often look to **peers for cues about appropriate behavior** rather than strictly optimizing decisions — a key institutional insight with direct ethical relevance

### Key Concepts: DiMaggio & Powell's Three Isomorphic Pressures

| Pressure | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Coercive** | Regulation, legal requirements, government mandates |
| **Mimetic** | Imitating perceived best practices under uncertainty |
| **Normative** | Professional norms, training, industry associations |

- **Scott's three pillars**: cultural-cognitive, normative, and regulative elements that provide stability and meaning to social life
- **Legitimacy** — the central currency organizations seek through conformity
- Recent research applies isomorphic pressures to **digital transformation** in municipalities and to corporate behavior (philanthropy, HRM, competitive strategy)

### IS Applications

- IT adoption and diffusion driven by legitimacy rather than pure efficiency
- E-government and digital transformation conformity pressures
- Explaining why organizations adopt ethics/AI governance frameworks (e.g., responsible-AI policies) mimetically and normatively, not merely coercively

## Governance, Risk and Compliance (GRC) <a name="governance-risk-and-compliance-grc"></a>

*Source: [IS Wiki — Governance, Risk and Compliance](https://is.theorizeit.org/wiki/Governance,_Risk_and_Compliance)*

### Origin & Key Authors

- A **managerial and IS concept** (not a formal theory) that emerged from auditing/finance practice (COSO, COBIT, ITIL) and became an integrated discipline in the 2000s
- Increasingly studied as a **digital infrastructure or practice field in IS research**
- Key IS-adjacent reference: **Sadiq & Governatori (2015)**, *"Managing regulatory compliance in business processes"*

### Core Idea

- **GRC is an integrated approach** through which organizations align their:
  - **Governance structures** — direction and accountability
  - **Risk management processes** — identification, assessment, mitigation
  - **Compliance efforts** — conformity with external regulatory demands and internal strategic goals
- Emerging IS research **critically examines** GRC's performative nature, **symbolic compliance**, and **sociotechnical tensions**

### Key Constructs (per the IS wiki)

| Independent constructs | Dependent constructs |
|---|---|
| Governance efficiency | Compliance effectiveness |
| Compliance management | Risk management performance |
| Risk mitigation | Operational resilience |
| Effective GRC systems | Organizational legitimacy |
| Regulatory complexity / IT integration capability | Audit readiness & user perception of compliance |

### Links to Other Theories (IS wiki)

- **Institutional Theory** → GRC adoption driven by legitimacy pressures
- **Sociotechnical Systems Theory** → misalignments in GRC system design and use
- **Structuration Theory** → GRC as duality of structure, shaping and shaped by organizational action
- **TAM** → user perception and adoption of GRC platforms
- **Critical IS Theory** → power, surveillance, symbolic compliance
